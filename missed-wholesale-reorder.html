What to Do When a Wholesale Customer Misses a Reorder

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Sales Leadership & Accounts · November 27, 2026

A purchasing manager who used to send a PO every month has gone quiet. Did a competitor get the order? Maybe. The buyer may also have six weeks of stock because their second shift stopped running. You can find out without making the customer defend a purchase they never promised you.

Start by looking at the item and the ship-to location. The head office might order something from you every Friday while one branch buys stretch film every five weeks. An account-level “days since last order” report can hide that branch for months. Compare like quantities, too. A double order before a shutdown will throw the next date off.

When should the next order have arrived?

In this fictional example, one branch ordered 24 cases of stretch film on January 6, February 3, March 3, and March 31, 2026. Those are three intervals of 28 calendar days. At the same quantity and pace, April 28 is a reasonable estimate for the next PO. On May 5, the branch is seven calendar days past that estimate. That’s a good reason to call.

Before you pick up the phone, check the last order. Did all 24 cases arrive together? Was there a backorder or return? Has another location been transferring cases into this one? Look at last spring as well. A seasonal packing line can make a neat four-week pattern disappear at exactly this point every year.

Give the buyer a question worth answering

I’d call with something specific. “Your north branch ordered 24 cases of film at the end of March. That’s usually about four weeks’ supply for that site. How’s the stock looking? I’m trying to get the timing right for your next run.” Then let the buyer answer before offering to write up an order.

Suppose the buyer says a line was down for maintenance and they still have 12 cases. They’re back to using six cases a week. That puts them about two weeks from running out. Their purchasing team wants to place the order a week ahead, so the rep offers to call May 12. Record the date and the buyer’s reason. The next rep who opens the account should see why a late April PO never came.

If the buyer did switch suppliers, ask which item moved and what drove the choice. They might still buy every other line from you. If you reach voicemail, leave the reason for the call and keep the outcome open. Silence alone gives you no basis for booking a loss.

Keep the follow-up list honest

Give each entry an expected PO date, the quantity behind that estimate, and the rep who will check it. Use a range for buyers with irregular orders and review seasonal accounts against the same selling period. When you roll the results into a distributor performance scorecard, count buyers who were actually due to order. A customer who buys one pallet every autumn has no reason to pull down your spring repeat-order rate.
