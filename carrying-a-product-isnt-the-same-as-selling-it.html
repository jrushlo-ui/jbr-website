Carrying a Product Isn’t the Same as Selling It

By Josh Rushlo · Published October 20, 2026 · 5 min read

The product is in your system. The opening order is on the shelf. Your company is listed as an authorized distributor.

What happens Monday morning?

If nobody can name the customers to approach, the reps responsible, or the next action, you have inventory and an agreement. You still need a sales plan.

In Part 1, More Distributors Doesn’t Mean More Sales, I focused on the manufacturer’s responsibility: choosing suitable partners, supporting their sales teams, creating demand, and following through. Those obligations matter. The distributor has commitments to make as well.

A productive partnership requires both sides to put people, time, and action behind the opportunity.

Be Honest About Why You Are Adding the Line

A distributor cannot actively promote every product it carries. Some lines support existing customer demand. Others fill a specific gap or represent a deliberate growth opportunity. Those are different relationships, and they require different expectations.

Problems start when a manufacturer believes it has recruited an active growth partner while the distributor simply intends to make the product available if someone asks.

Before adding a line, explain what you can realistically commit. Which customers fit? What opportunity does it create for your business? How much attention can your team give it? What support will you need from the manufacturer?

There is nothing wrong with a limited stocking relationship when both sides understand it. Promising active development and delivering passive availability is where trust breaks down.

Put a Person Behind the Commitment

“Our sales team will get behind it” sounds encouraging. It leaves a lot unanswered.

Someone inside the distributor needs to coordinate the effort, connect the right reps with the manufacturer, and keep agreed actions moving. That person also needs enough support from leadership to make the commitment credible.

If managers discuss the new line once and never return to it, reps have little reason to believe it is a priority. Give it an appropriate place in sales meetings, coaching, and account planning. Help the team understand how it contributes to customer relationships and profitable business.

The manufacturer should have a clear contact. Your reps should know where to get help. And leadership should know who is responsible when activity stalls.

Turn Product Training Into Customer Conversations

Attending a product presentation is useful preparation. The value comes when someone applies it.

End training with a practical next step. Have each participating rep identify a few suitable accounts and explain why the product might matter to those customers. Then arrange the introductions, samples, demonstrations, or joint calls needed to test the opportunity.

For example, a distributor adding a complementary product could start with customers already purchasing the related category. The first conversation should explore how they handle that need today, what problems they encounter, and whether a different option deserves consideration.

That is more useful than broadcasting a new-product announcement to the entire customer list and waiting for orders.

Bring the manufacturer into the process where it adds value. Ask for help with an application or objection your team cannot answer. A joint call should help the rep build the relationship and learn, with clear follow-up afterward.

Make Follow-Up Part of the Sale

A sample shipped without a follow-up date can disappear into a customer’s daily workload. A quote sent without a next conversation can sit unanswered. A first order can remain a one-time purchase if nobody checks the result.

Agree on the next step with the customer. Who will evaluate the product? What does a successful result look like? When will you reconnect? After the order, ask whether the product met the need and whether there is a reason to expand its use.

The same discipline applies to manufacturer-generated leads. Acknowledge them, qualify the opportunity, and report what happened. If the lead is unsuitable, explain why. If you cannot work it, say so early enough for both sides to find a workable response.

Consistent follow-up gives the partnership something concrete to build on.

Give the Manufacturer Feedback It Can Act On

“The price is too high” or “Nobody is asking for it” may describe a real obstacle. Neither gives the manufacturer much direction on its own.

Useful feedback explains the situation: the customer’s application, the alternative being considered, the commercial gap, and what would need to change for the opportunity to advance.

Separate what you heard from what you assume. A customer choosing a familiar product may have concerns about switching, availability, or support. A lower price will not automatically resolve those concerns.

Be equally direct about problems the manufacturer owns. If quotes take too long, training misses the customer’s questions, or delivery commitments are unreliable, bring specific examples and ask for a clear corrective action. Honest feedback is part of contributing to the relationship.

Review Progress Before Everyone Gets Frustrated

A useful partnership review looks at customers approached, qualified opportunities, first orders, repeat purchases, margin, and inventory movement. Choose the measures that match the product and selling cycle.

Discuss actions on both sides. Did the manufacturer deliver the promised support? Did your reps make the agreed calls? What did customers say? What will each side do next, and by when?

If the line is not gaining traction, diagnose the issue. It may be poor customer fit, weak execution, insufficient support, or a change in the market. Those call for different responses. More inventory will not solve all of them.

Sometimes the honest answer is that the line does not deserve a larger commitment. Have that conversation and reset expectations rather than allowing an inactive relationship to drift.

The Agreement Is the Starting Point

Manufacturers need to earn attention and provide dependable support. Distributors need to turn their customer access into focused selling activity. Both need to deliver on what they promise.

Putting a product on the shelf makes it available. Putting a plan and people behind it gives the partnership a chance to grow.

Look at one line your company added recently. Can you name its internal owner, the customers you are developing, the next customer action, and the support you need from the manufacturer?

If those answers are clear, you have something you can manage and improve. If they are missing, that is a useful place to begin.
