How to Fix the Sales-to-Operations Handoff

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Sales & Operations · November 25, 2026

Imagine a buyer calling to ask why all 80 coils of hose went to the north branch. Your rep pulls up the email where the buyer asked for 40 at each branch. Your shipping lead pulls up the order. One address. Eighty coils. Now the buyer has to find a truck to move half the order across town.

As a sales leader, I want to be able to ask how that happened. The shipping lead ought to be able to ask me why an instruction that important was sitting in a rep’s inbox. If either question sounds like somebody stepping out of their lane, the people who need to fix the handoff have just been told to stay away from it.

What sales and operations leaders need to decide together

A sales-to-operations handoff is complete when someone in operations has checked what the customer bought, accepted responsibility for delivering it, and made any unresolved promise visible to sales. Marking a deal “won” tells you the buyer said yes. It tells the warehouse very little about what that yes was based on.

The sales leader owns the accuracy of the promise. That means coaching reps to capture the scope, exclusions, quantities, locations, and dates the buyer is counting on. The operations leader owns the delivery plan, including who does the work, in what sequence, and with what capacity. The two leaders need to agree on what a rep can promise from standard lead times, what needs an operations check before the quote goes out, and who can approve an exception.

Here’s the distinction that matters to me. A rep can ask, “Can I tell the buyer their crew will have the material by Thursday morning?” Operations gets to answer that question and choose how to deliver. If the rep promises Thursday first, the warehouse inherits a deadline it never agreed to. If operations later learns Thursday will slip and keeps that information on the dock, sales leaves a crew waiting. Both leaders have a stake in fixing either failure.

What a good handoff looks like on an $8,400 order

Take Harbor Tool, the fictional buyer from the weekly sales pipeline review. Suppose Harbor is ready to buy the hoses. Its north branch can receive 40 coils on Tuesday. Its south branch needs the other 40 by noon Thursday, when its receiving dock closes. Here’s how that order should travel.

  1. Before the promise. Marcus, the rep, checks both arrival windows with shipping. The shipping lead confirms stock and carrier availability for each address. Only then does Marcus confirm the dates with Harbor.
  2. When the purchase order arrives. Marcus checks it against the accepted quote. On the sales order he records the hose specification, quantity for each branch, both ship-to addresses, receiving contacts, and the agreed arrival windows. He attaches the buyer’s instructions so the next person can check the source.
  3. When operations accepts the work. The shipping lead creates two releases, one per branch, and checks that each pick ticket carries the right quantity and destination. Marcus can see both confirmed arrivals on the order. If the second address fails to print on the pick ticket, the lead holds the release and works out the split with Marcus before a label gets printed.

When keeping one promise could break another

On Wednesday, the carrier moves South’s Thursday delivery to the afternoon. The dock will be closed. Dispatch could put Harbor on the company truck Thursday morning, but doing that would bump another customer’s confirmed delivery. This is the decision that gets lost when the two department heads run separate meetings.

The operations lead gets a price for a dedicated morning delivery and confirms a Friday slot on the regular route. The sales lead asks Marcus what Friday would cost Harbor. It turns out the buyer has an installation crew booked for Thursday. Now the two leaders can weigh the extra freight against the margin on the order and the consequence for both customers.

In this example, they approve the extra freight within their authority. Operations books the morning run and updates the release. Marcus calls Harbor with the confirmed arrival and checks that it still works for the crew. The other customer keeps its slot. Had Friday worked for Harbor, Marcus could have recorded the buyer’s agreement and saved the freight. A backorder calls for the same early conversation about what can ship and who pays the freight.

What to look at together each week

Start with one order that came back for clarification and one that missed a customer commitment. Put the accepted quote, the order, and the delivery record side by side. Ask where the facts first diverged, who could see the problem at that point, and what change would have caught it. If a receiving cutoff vanished between the quote and the pick ticket, fix that field and check the next split order together.

Two numbers are worth bringing to that conversation. Time from PO receipt to an accepted, booked order shows how long work waits between teams. The share of orders delivered in full by the date the buyer originally accepted tells you how often you kept the promise. Keep any revised date and the reason beside the original. Both leaders should see the same numbers and be able to explain a miss together.

Try this on the last order that went sideways. If the rep had taken the day off, could the shipping lead have found exactly what the buyer expected? And if the truck had missed its window, would the rep have known in time to call the buyer? Those answers tell you where to start.
