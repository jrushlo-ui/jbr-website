When Should a Manufacturer Give a Distributor an Exclusive Territory?

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Distributor Channels · November 25, 2026

When a distributor asks for the whole state, I want to know which call I’ll have to turn down if I say yes. Suppose a strong dealer in the western half calls next Tuesday with a customer ready to buy. Can the partner asking for exclusivity serve that customer? Who would make the visit, from which branch, and how soon?

A manufacturer has a good commercial reason to protect a distributor that commits people, inventory, or service capacity to develop a market. The size of the commitment should give you a way to judge the size of the territory.

What has the distributor earned?

Ask for a map of end customers who actually bought the line in the last six months. Mark which ones bought again, which rep called on each account, and where the distributor can deliver or service the product on its promised schedule. Then ask what the partner would invest if you granted the protection. A named rep, a stocked branch, and a training date are commitments you can come back and check.

Look at the economics on both sides. A partner carrying spares and sending a technician on startup visits has a real cost to recover. The manufacturer gives up the opportunity to add another reseller in the protected market. An opening inventory purchase alone tells you little about how many buyers that trade will reach.

A four-county request with evidence for two

Here’s a fictional decision. A distributor wants exclusive coverage of independent repair shops across four counties for a compressor accessory line. It has sold the line to 18 shops. Fifteen are in the two counties surrounding its branch, and all six shops that reordered are there. The remaining three accounts were inbound orders. Nobody has been assigned to visit either outer county.

I’d offer a six-month pilot covering independent repair shops in the two counties the branch can reach. Define the product line and put the existing manufacturer-served accounts on a shared list before new leads start coming in. If the distributor is worried about losing one named project outside the pilot area, use deal registration to address that project.

For this pilot, the partner agrees to stock service parts, assign an outside rep, and respond to referred shops within two business days. The manufacturer agrees to route qualifying inquiries to that rep and provide technical training. At 90 days, review lead response and actual visits together. At six months, check whether eight additional shops bought and whether four eligible new buyers returned. Agree up front on what makes a buyer eligible based on the line’s reorder cycle.

Make expansion a separate decision

If the first two counties perform, ask the partner to show who will cover the third and what that person can realistically carry. If results fall short, check whether both sides kept their commitments before deciding to renew, narrow, or end the pilot. A territory map is much easier to discuss when you can point to the customer work behind each boundary. That’s a question I work through with manufacturers in channel strategy consulting.
