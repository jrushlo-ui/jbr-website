How to Estimate Distributor Sell-Through From Inventory and Shipments

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Distributor Channels · November 25, 2026

Your distributor bought 70 cases last month. How many did it sell? It could have sold 30 and stacked the rest in the warehouse. It could have sold 100 by drawing down stock it already had. Your invoices tell you how much you shipped to the distributor. You need a count at both ends of the month to work out what left its shelves.

Reconcile one SKU at one location

Pick an item and use the distributor’s opening and closing saleable inventory for the same branch and dates. Choose one unit of measure for everything. If the warehouse counts cases of 12 and your invoice shows pieces, convert first. Use receipts posted by the distributor during the period. A truck you dispatched on the last day of the month may still be on the road when the branch closes its books.

Start with opening stock. Add supplier receipts, transfers in, and customer returns put back into saleable stock. Subtract transfers out, supplier returns, write-offs from saleable stock, and the closing count. What’s left is your estimate of units shipped to external customers, provided you accounted for the other stock movements.

How a branch sold an estimated 100 net cases of sealant

Take a fictional branch’s October report for one sealant SKU. Every number below is in cases. All five customer returns went back into saleable stock.

  • The branch opened with 100 cases and received 70 from the manufacturer.
  • Another branch transferred 12 cases in. Customers returned 5 cases.
  • The branch transferred 8 cases out, returned 3 to the supplier, and wrote off 4 damaged cases.
  • The closing count was 67 saleable cases.

Estimated gross customer shipments were 105 cases. The calculation is 100 + 70 + 12 + 5 − 8 − 3 − 4 − 67 = 105. Subtract the five customer returns and you get 100 net cases sold for October. A return that bypasses saleable stock still belongs in the customer returns record when you calculate net sales.

You can check the result from the other direction. Start with 100, add the 70 receipts and 12 inbound transfers, then subtract 8 outbound transfers, 3 supplier returns, 4 write-offs, and 100 net customer sales. That leaves the 67 cases the branch counted.

What decision can you make with that number?

The manufacturer can see that customer demand drew on inventory already at the branch. Before recommending another 70-case shipment, I’d ask which orders are open, how long the next delivery takes, and whether October included a one-time project. A physical count can’t tell you which customer bought, what price they paid, or whether they’ll buy again. An unrecorded stock adjustment can also look like a sale.

If a movement is missing, label the figure provisional and ask the branch to reconcile it. Use transaction-level sales or point-of-sale data when you need to verify actual customer purchases. For the next distributor quarterly business review, bring manufacturer shipments and estimated net sell-through as separately labeled figures. Sorting out what each partner can actually measure is part of managing a distributor network.
