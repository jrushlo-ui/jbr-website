More Distributors Doesn’t Mean More Sales

By Josh Rushlo · Published October 19, 2026 · 5 min read

A new distributor signs on. The opening order ships. Another territory gets marked on the map. It feels like growth.

Then the reorder doesn’t come. The sales team moves on to recruiting the next partner, while the last one sits on inventory. The network gets bigger. Sales barely move.

Signing a distributor creates access to a market. Turning that access into revenue takes work from both sides.

I’ve seen both sides of this problem: partners who agree to carry a product but never commit meaningful sales effort, and manufacturers who sign a partner and fail to support the relationship. This first article focuses on what manufacturers can control. Part two will address the distributor’s responsibility.

An Opening Order Is Only the First Step

An opening order is a real sale, but it doesn’t tell you whether the distributor can build a repeatable business around your product. That becomes clearer when the product moves to customers, those customers come back, and the distributor reorders.

If your channel review stops at how many accounts you opened or how much inventory you shipped, you can miss what happens next. Ask which partners are reaching new customers, which are generating repeat business, and which are still sitting on their initial purchase.

That distinction should shape where you put your time. A small partner developing customers may deserve more attention than a large name that has made one purchase and gone quiet.

Choose a Partner Who Can Win With Your Product

Geographic coverage is useful. So is a recognizable name. Neither tells you how your product will fit into that distributor’s daily business.

Before signing, get specific. Which customers would buy? Which reps call on them? What does your product add to the distributor’s offering? Who inside the organization will take responsibility for developing the line?

Consider the rep’s perspective, too. They already have products they understand, customers who trust them, and demands on their time. Your offering needs a clear commercial reason to earn attention: a customer problem it solves, an account it helps them win, or profitable business they are currently missing.

If neither side can explain that opportunity clearly, another agreement won’t fix it.

Give Reps Something They Can Use in a Customer Conversation

A catalog, price sheet, and introductory presentation can support a launch. The rep still needs to know how to use them to sell.

Useful training helps someone recognize an opportunity and take the next step. It should answer a few practical questions:

  • Which customers should I approach first?
  • What problem or buying situation makes this product relevant?
  • What should I ask to find out whether there is a fit?
  • How do I explain its value and handle the likely objections?
  • Who will help with a sample, demonstration, technical question, or quote?

Then put that training to work. Choose a short list of target accounts together. Join customer calls. Help the first opportunities move forward and use what you learn to improve the next conversation.

Make the basics dependable as well. Slow answers, unclear pricing, and unreliable availability make it harder for a rep to put their customer relationship behind your product.

Keep Creating Demand After the Agreement Is Signed

A distributor brings customer relationships, local knowledge, inventory, and selling capacity. The manufacturer still has a role in giving customers a reason to buy.

That might mean developing qualified leads, supporting demonstrations, supplying credible product information, or working with the distributor on a focused campaign. The appropriate approach depends on the product and market. The responsibility needs to be explicit.

Agree on how leads will be routed, who will follow up, and how both sides will see progress. If the manufacturer assumes the distributor will create all the demand, while the distributor expects the manufacturer to deliver ready-to-buy customers, the relationship starts with a gap that enthusiasm won’t close.

Review Commitments on Both Sides

“How are sales?” is a weak substitute for managing a partnership.

A useful review starts with the plan: the accounts being pursued, opportunities in progress, obstacles, and actions each side agreed to complete. If the manufacturer promised training or samples, review whether those arrived. If the distributor committed to customer visits, review what happened and what comes next.

Use a small set of measures that fits the business: active buying customers, repeat orders, qualified opportunities, profitable sales, and inventory movement where the data is available. Give every next step an owner and a date.

Support doesn’t remove the need for accountability. When a partner repeatedly fails to follow through despite a clear opportunity and reasonable support, have that conversation. Understand whether the issue is capability, capacity, or commitment, and decide whether the relationship still makes sense.

Before You Add the Next Distributor

Review the partners you already have. Can you name the person responsible for your line, the customers you are targeting together, and the next action your own team owes them?

If those answers are missing, expanding the network risks repeating the same problem across more accounts. Fixing the way you develop existing partners may be the most useful place to start.

Before adding your next distributor, ask what would happen if you helped your existing partners sell more.

There are good reasons to expand distribution: reaching an underserved market, adding a capability, or meeting demand your current network cannot serve. Be clear about the gap you are filling and how the new partnership will produce results.
