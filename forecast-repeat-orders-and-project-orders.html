How to Forecast Repeat Orders and Project Orders Without Double Counting

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Sales Leadership · November 25, 2026

Your regular customer orders bearings every month. This month the same customer is also replacing a production line. Your territory rep has the project in the pipeline, purchasing has already released part of it, and the branch manager’s forecast still includes a full month of bearings. All three records may be accurate. Add the wrong numbers together and you’ll tell the owner to expect orders the branch has already booked.

For a monthly order bookings forecast, add orders already accepted this month, routine orders you still expect this month, and project orders you still expect this month. Give each order line one place in that calculation. A shipment against a purchase order accepted last month belongs in the delivery plan. It won’t generate a second booking when the truck leaves.

A branch with $102,000 on the books

Here’s a fictional November forecast for an industrial distributor. Based on account buying patterns, the branch expects $120,000 in routine replenishment orders for the whole month. The figure excludes project purchases. By midmonth it has accepted $82,000 of those routine orders. The rep checks that the remaining $38,000 still fits the buyers’ current plans.

There’s also a $50,000 line-replacement project. Purchasing has released a $20,000 order against that quote. The buyer expects to release another $12,000 in November and the remaining $18,000 in December. The branch uses the same order values throughout, excluding tax and freight.

November bookings forecast at midmonth
Order typeAlready bookedStill expected in NovemberNovember forecast
Routine replenishment$82,000$38,000$120,000
Line-replacement project$20,000$12,000$32,000
Total$102,000$50,000$152,000

Notice what happens if you add the $102,000 already booked, the full $120,000 repeat forecast, and the original $50,000 project quote. You get $272,000. That counts the $82,000 of replenishment and the $20,000 project release twice, and pulls December’s $18,000 into November. Those three errors explain the entire $120,000 gap.

What changes when the next PO comes in?

Say the next $12,000 project release arrives Thursday. Match its lines to the project quote and move that amount from expected to booked. Actual bookings rise to $114,000. Expected November bookings drop to $38,000. The month still comes to $152,000. If instead the buyer pushes that release into December, November falls to $140,000. A changed customer commitment should move your forecast. Recording a PO you already expected should change how much of the number you can count on.

What to ask the rep on Friday

Start with the largest project in the weekly pipeline review. Ask which PO lines the branch has accepted, which releases the buyer still expects this month, and whether any of those items are also in the account’s normal reorder estimate. Keep the account, quote reference, PO line, and expected release month together. If purchasing buys a project item on its usual blanket PO, the line’s purpose still determines which bucket it belongs in.

If your forecast keeps growing every time a rep enters an order, pick one account and reconcile it this way. That’s a good place to start a conversation about improving the sales process at your branch.
