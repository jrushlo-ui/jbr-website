How to Build a Key Account Growth Plan for a Distributor

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Sales Leadership · November 25, 2026

Pull up one of your largest customers. You can probably say what they bought last year. Can you name one purchase they make somewhere else, who gets to choose that supplier, and why the buyer would consider a change? That’s where I’d start a key account growth plan.

A useful plan fits on one page. Pick a location and a product category, verify the buying need with the customer, and agree on the next decision. “Grow the account 15%” gives the rep a target. It still leaves them wondering whom to call on Monday.

Find the gap in what the customer buys

Group the last 12 months of invoices by ship-to location and product family. Check for alternate account numbers before treating an empty cell as an opportunity. A branch may buy centrally, use a different specification, or have six months left on a supply agreement. Ask the person who receives or uses the product how it gets ordered today.

Three plants buy cartons. One also buys labels.

Imagine a fictional distributor selling shipping cartons to three plants operated by one customer. East also buys thermal labels. North and South buy their labels elsewhere. A conversation with purchasing confirms North uses about 35 rolls a month and South uses 25. At an illustrative $18 per roll, the two locations together represent $12,960 in possible annual sales if you win all the business at that price. East’s existing purchases are already in your sales history.

There’s a catch. South is covered by a corporate supply agreement through year-end. North can choose its supplier, but its shipping supervisor has had trouble with labels peeling off cartons in cold storage. Now the rep has a reason to call North and a reason to leave South alone for the moment.

What goes on North’s account plan

  • Opportunity. North’s 35 rolls a month would come to $7,560 a year at the example price. That figure describes the size of the opportunity. Actual sales start with a purchase order.
  • Buying decision. The shipping supervisor evaluates adhesion and printer compatibility. Purchasing can approve a supplier once the trial passes and it accepts the delivered price.
  • Next move. The rep confirms the printer model and carton surface on Tuesday. If there’s a suitable sample, the supervisor agrees to test it on the next cold-storage shipment. They book a call for the day after receiving to compare notes.
  • Evidence of progress. Record the trial result, the buyer’s decision on the price, and the date of the first paid order. Check the next month to see whether North reorders. Give South a separate reminder ahead of its supplier review.

At the next account review, ask the rep what the customer did since the last conversation. A successful label trial earns a discussion about a first order. A failed trial earns a product question. Both give the sales leader a better coaching conversation than asking when the whole $12,960 will close. That’s the kind of work I focus on in account management coaching.
