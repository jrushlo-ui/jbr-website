Which Sales Workflow Should a Distributor Automate First?

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Sales Technology · November 25, 2026

It’s 11:15. A buyer calls about a request for quote they sent at eight. One rep thought the branch manager had picked it up. The branch manager thought it went straight to the account rep. Three people have seen the email and the buyer is still waiting.

If that happens at your branch, I’d automate getting incoming requests for quote (RFQs) to the rep who owns the account. You can tell whether it worked by opening the inbox and the rep’s work queue. If your branch already handles that well, look for another task with the same qualities. It happens often, your team agrees on the rule, and someone can catch a mistake before it reaches a customer.

What one week at the sales desk can tell you

Consider a fictional industrial distributor choosing among three jobs its inside sales team does every day. For five business days, the team tallies each request and the minutes people spend handling it. These are illustrative numbers for one branch.

Three candidates for a first automation
Task and weekly workloadWhat the system could do
Assign incoming RFQs
54 requests, 190 minutes		Send each clear account match to its assigned rep. The rep reads the request and owns the quote.
Route discount exceptions
12 requests, 96 minutes		Gather the proposed price, cost, and reason for the exception. The sales manager decides whether the margin makes sense.
Answer delivery-date questions
9 requests, 54 minutes		Send the question to shipping with the order number. Operations confirms what can arrive when, and the rep answers the buyer.

Of the 54 RFQs, 40 contained a customer number that matched one active account with one assigned rep. At three minutes apiece, those assignments took 120 minutes. The other 14 took 70 minutes. Some came from new buyers. One purchasing office ordered for branches in two territories. An experienced rep had to ask which branch was buying. That question is part of serving the account.

I’d pick RFQ routing here. The 40 clear matches give the team a clean test and an upper limit of two hours a week it might recover at this volume. The 70 minutes of judgment stay in the budget, along with whatever time it takes to check and correct the automation.

Where the automation stops

Imagine request 418 comes in with customer number 2076. The account record lists Dana as the rep. A rule in the existing CRM creates one task in Dana’s queue with the time received and a link to the original request. Dana checks the hose specification and calls the buyer. The rule can read the designated quote inbox and account-owner list and create that internal task. Sending email, setting prices, and changing account ownership stay with the people who do that work today.

If two reps claim the account, the request goes to a named inside sales lead for review. If that happens often, work through the account ownership rules before turning on routing. A customer number and a reliable owner field are enough for the clear matches. You can save AI for a job that actually needs it, such as extracting a part description from an email for a rep to verify.

How to decide whether to keep it

For the first week, let the rule suggest owners while the team still assigns the work. Compare the suggestions with the actual assignments. Fix mismatches before letting the rule create tasks. During the live pilot, the inside sales lead checks each day that every new request appears once, either with a rep or in the manual queue.

For this branch, I’d keep it if the next 40 eligible requests take no more than 60 minutes of staff time, including checks and corrections, and every RFQ reaches the right person. That would recover at least an hour against the 120-minute baseline. If a request disappears or goes to someone without account access, pause the rule and use the shared inbox until the cause is fixed.

Bring me one ordinary week of work and the tasks your reps keep chasing. That’s where I’d start a conversation about AI and workflow consulting for sales teams.
