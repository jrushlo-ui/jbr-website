How to Set Discount Approval Rules for a Distribution Sales Team

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Sales Operations · November 25, 2026

A rep calls from a buyer’s parking lot. “If I take ten percent off, they’ll sign today. Can I do it?” If your answer depends on who called, how badly the branch needs the order, or whether you have time to open the quote, your reps have good reason to keep asking.

Give them a rule they can use while the buyer is still on the phone. Start with the margin at the proposed price, spell out who can approve an exception, and tell the rep what information that person needs to decide.

What ten percent off does to a distributor’s margin

Take a fictional fitting with a landed cost of $70. At the standard $100 selling price, it earns $30 gross profit per unit, a 30% gross margin. Offer it at $95 and the gross profit falls to $25. Divide $25 by the new selling price of $95 and the margin is 26.3%. At the buyer’s proposed $90, the gross profit is $20 and the margin is 22.2%.

Here’s what I’d want the rep to see. An order for 100 units at $100 earns $3,000 gross profit. At $90, it takes 150 units to earn the same $3,000, assuming the same unit cost and freight terms. “We’ll buy more down the road” leaves a pretty big hole in this order.

Who can say yes at each price?

Suppose this distributor uses the following approval levels for standard fittings. These are illustrative. Set your own levels against the economics of each product group, including which costs are already in the margin calculation.

  • 26% gross margin or higher. The rep can quote standard payment and freight terms. The $95 offer qualifies.
  • 23% up to, but below, 26%. The branch sales manager can approve. The rep brings the quantity, the buyer’s reason for asking, and what the buyer will commit to in return.
  • Below 23% or any nonstandard terms. The pricing lead reviews the full offer before it goes to the customer. Free freight, extended terms, and a special rebate can change the decision even when the unit price clears the margin floor.

Calculate approval levels using the unrounded margin. Write down who covers for the manager and pricing lead when they’re out. A rule that leaves a rep waiting until Monday for a Friday bid is going to get worked around.

Give the buyer a real choice

Back in the parking lot, the rep can offer $95 now. If the buyer needs $90, the next question is, “Would you put 150 units on this purchase order with one delivery?” If that works, the rep takes the written quantity and shipping terms to the pricing lead. The rep can give the buyer a time for an answer without promising a price that still needs approval.

Record the approved price, quantity, terms, approver, and expiration on the quote. At the next sales meeting, pull two exceptions and compare what the buyer committed to with what they actually ordered. That’s a worthwhile conversation for sales managers coaching their reps on price.
