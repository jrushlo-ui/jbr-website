How to Explain a Price Increase to a Wholesale Customer

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Sales Operations · November 25, 2026

When a wholesale buyer opens a price-increase notice, they’re probably thinking about an order they already placed or a price they already gave their own customer. An email saying “effective March 1” still leaves them digging through their open quotes. Tell them what changes on their account and which commitments they can still count on.

Check the account before writing the notice

Pull the affected item numbers, old and new prices, selling unit, and effective date. Check which event triggers the change. An order placed in February and shipped in March can have two different answers depending on whether your approved policy uses order date or ship date. Look up any account-specific agreement, open order acknowledgments, and outstanding quotes. Get pricing or management to resolve differences before you give the buyer a number.

If you explain why the price changed, use a reason you’ve checked and can stand behind. Buyers remember being told a surcharge was temporary. Be ready to say which charges or terms change along with the unit price, especially if the buyer compares your total delivered cost.

An email a buyer can forward to purchasing

Here’s a fictional example. The rep has verified the account’s prices and exceptions with the pricing lead. The customer buys two filter packs and has both an accepted order and an unexpired quote for the affected item.

Subject — March 1 price on your FB-10 filter packs

Hi Morgan,

Starting March 1, 2027, new orders for FB-10 filter packs will be $51 per case, up from $48. Your FB-20 packs stay at $64 per case. Your freight terms stay the same.

I checked the two items you already have in progress. PO 4187 stays at $48 per case, including the balance shipping in March. Quote 622 covers 20 more cases at $48 each if you place that order by its March 12 expiration. For new FB-10 orders outside that quote, we’ll use the price in effect on the day we receive the order.

If this affects a bid you have out, send me the quantity and the date you need an answer. I’ll check what options we have.

Alex

Handle the first objection with the numbers in front of you

Suppose Morgan replies, “I already bid another 50 cases at the old price.” At $3 more per case, that’s a $150 difference on 50 cases outside the open quote. Now you have something specific to discuss. Ask whether the bid has been awarded, how firm the quantity is, and when the customer expects to release the order.

If you can request an exception, take that quantity, timing, and dollar impact to the person with approval authority. Give the buyer a time you’ll call back. If the price has to stand, say so plainly. A substitute can be worth reviewing if it meets the buyer’s specification and they approve the change.

Before the next release, have inside sales open the account and read back the new price, the grandfathered order, and the quote expiration. That small handoff between sales and operations can spare the buyer a second conversation about the very price you just explained.
