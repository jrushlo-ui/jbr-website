How to Build a Distributor Sales Channel

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Channel Strategy · Published September 19, 2026

Before you call the next distributor, write down the customers you need to reach and why your current sales model is missing them. A longer partner list will not fix an unclear route to market.

A useful distributor sales channel connects three things: a customer need, a partner able to serve it, and a plan both businesses will carry out. Here is how I would work through that plan.

1. Define the Customer and Coverage Gap

Be specific about the buyer, application, geography, and service requirement. “We need more distribution in the Midwest” leaves too much open. “We need access to independent contractors in these territories, with local stock and application support” gives you something to evaluate.

Look at current customers, lost opportunities, delivery constraints, and gaps in your direct-sales coverage. Decide what a distributor needs to contribute beyond accepting an opening order.

2. Evaluate Partner Fit Before Discussing Exclusivity

A distributor with a large customer list may still give your line very little attention. Ask how the product fits its customers, current assortment, rep workload, and commercial priorities.

  • Which customers would the partner approach first, and why?
  • Who inside the distributor would own the line?
  • What competing or complementary products does the team already sell?
  • What stock, technical support, and training would the partner need?
  • Will leadership commit sales time to developing the business?

Listen for named people, suitable accounts, and practical next steps. Broad enthusiasm is useful, but it does not tell you how the product will reach a customer.

3. Work Through the Economics and Service Expectations

Review the distributor’s room to earn a reasonable return alongside your own cost to support the account. Consider inventory commitments, freight, order size, payment terms, returns, and the work required before and after an order.

A product that needs substantial application support may require a different partner model from a familiar item customers already request. Resolve those expectations while both sides are deciding whether the relationship fits.

4. Agree on Who Owns the Customer Work

Clarify account ownership, lead handling, quote follow-up, joint calls, and how the channel works alongside direct sales. Give each partner a clear contact inside your company and a way to raise a problem.

For example, if your team identifies an end-user opportunity, agree on who introduces the distributor, who handles the technical discussion, and who reports the next customer action. A handoff should include a person and an agreed next step.

These decisions also help prevent conflict between direct sales and distributors.

5. Turn Onboarding into an Account Plan

Use the first training session to prepare for actual customer conversations. Pick a manageable group of suitable accounts. Identify the rep, the customer need, the support required, and the date of the next action for each one.

A simple working plan can cover:

  • Preparation: confirm the internal owner, product availability, ordering process, and training needs.
  • Customer activity: agree on target accounts, introductions, joint calls, demonstrations, or samples.
  • Follow-through: review what customers said, what advanced, and where support is missing.

The timing should fit the product and buying cycle. An opening order is a milestone; the next test is whether the partner can create repeatable customer demand.

6. Review Activity Alongside Sales

Choose measures that explain progress. Revenue and margin matter, but they do not always show why a new line is stalling. Review qualified opportunities, first orders, repeat purchases, stock movement, and agreed actions where those measures fit the business.

Ask what each side committed to do and what actually happened. If your company missed a quote, shipment, or training commitment, address that as directly as you would a distributor’s missed follow-up.

For an established network, start by reviewing existing partners before expanding. More Distributors Doesn’t Mean More Sales explains that decision in more detail.

A Useful Check Before You Add the Next Partner

Can you name the customers the partner will develop, the people responsible, the support your company owes them, and the way you will review progress? If those answers are missing, finish that work before counting the new agreement as growth.

A distributor agreement opens a door. The account plan and follow-through determine what happens next.
