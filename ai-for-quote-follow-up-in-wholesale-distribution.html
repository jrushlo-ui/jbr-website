AI for Quote Follow-Up in Wholesale Distribution

By Josh Rushlo · JBR Insights · Sales Technology · November 25, 2026

“Any thoughts on the quote?” The buyer already gave you one. They asked whether the hose could get to two branches on different days. If you send that email, the buyer has to ask the same question twice.

That’s the test I’d give any AI tool drafting quote emails for a distributor. Can it pick out the question the customer is waiting on? If your team has the answer, the draft should lead with it. If somebody still has to call shipping, the rep can tell the buyer who’s checking and when they’ll hear back.

One hose quote, two different emails

Take Harbor Tool, the fictional customer from the weekly sales pipeline review. Marcus quoted $8,400 on Q-1851. The buyer wants 40 coils at North on Tuesday, December 1, and 40 at South by noon Thursday, December 3. South’s receiving dock closes at noon. An email promising to ship on Thursday could leave 40 coils sitting on a truck outside a locked door.

While shipping is checking

Marcus has the request and the quote. He still needs to confirm stock, both arrival windows, and whether the split changes the price. He can commit to getting the buyer an update by noon Tuesday, November 17. Here’s all the first email needs to say.

I’ve got your quantities and the noon cutoff at South. I’m checking whether we can deliver 40 coils to North on December 1 and the other 40 to South before the dock closes on December 3. I’m checking the freight on that split, too. I’ll update you by noon tomorrow, November 17, even if I’m still waiting on the carrier.

Marcus can send that in the existing quote thread. He owns the next move. Asking the buyer to hop on a call would just give them another job.

Once the rep can ask for the order

In this example, shipping confirms stock and carrier coverage for both arrivals. Marcus verifies that $8,400 covers the split, and order entry confirms it needs the purchase order by noon Central on November 19 to meet those dates. Those three facts change the email.

Yes, we can deliver 40 coils to North on Tuesday, December 1, and 40 to South before noon Thursday, December 3. The total for both deliveries is $8,400, as quoted.

If that works for both branches, please send the PO against Q-1851 by noon Central on Thursday, November 19. I’ll send you the order acknowledgment with both delivery windows once it’s booked.

If the PO cutoff passes without a response, Marcus checks availability again before offering those dates to Harbor. If the buyer places the order, both receiving windows belong on the handoff to shipping. The warehouse needs the same instructions the buyer just accepted.

A prompt your reps can use on an open quote

Use a company-approved tool. A quote ID, the buyer’s question, and a few relevant notes will usually do. Paste the following above those details.

Draft a short reply to the buyer’s last question using the quote and notes below. First, give me a private list of anything I still need to verify. Then write the customer email. Use only documented quantities, prices, freight terms, stock, deadlines, and arrival dates. Preserve the difference between shipping and arrival.

If the answer is pending, say what I’m checking. Give an update time only if I supplied one. If the terms are confirmed, answer the question and ask for the next buying decision. Leave out any promise the notes don’t support.

Before sending, read the draft against the quote and the latest shipping note. Could you stand behind every date and dollar amount if the buyer forwarded it to their purchasing manager? That’s the same standard I’d use for an email the rep wrote from scratch.

If your reps spend more time chasing the facts behind a quote than writing the email, that’s a good place to start a conversation about AI consulting for sales teams.
